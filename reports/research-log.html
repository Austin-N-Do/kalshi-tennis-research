Quantitative research log · Kalshi tennis markets

Five consecutive null results

A prediction-market trading system, and the research programme built to stop it fooling its author. Every hypothesis below was preregistered with a pass/fail rule fixed before the data existed. Most of them failed.

Python 3.12 · FastAPI · PostgreSQL · Redis · XGBoost 1,435 settled paper trades 8 concurrent cohorts Jul – Sep 2026

The interesting artifact here is not a profitable strategy. It is the machinery that established, five separate times, that a strategy I wanted to believe in did not work.

A backtest engine in this repo once reported +11.8% ROI for a zero-skill model, because a single line asserted every position won. It is quarantined, and the incident is why everything below is preregistered.

The system

What it does, in one paragraph.

A live scanner prices ATP, WTA and ITF tennis matches on Kalshi, an order-driven prediction exchange. An Elo-plus-serve model and a Monte-Carlo match simulator produce a win probability; the system compares it against the executable quote — the ask actually lifted, never a midpoint — and places paper trades through eight independent cohorts that differ by one variable each. Positions are managed by three exit rules (stop-loss, model-shift, take-profit) and graded automatically against Kalshi settlement.

The cohort design is the point. Each pair differs in exactly one rule, so any comparison is a live A/B rather than a backtest.

Two findings worth the detail

One that shrank under scrutiny, one still under test.

A $691 result that turned out to be a fee

Across 170 take-profit exits, holding to settlement would have beaten selling by $691. Splitting by date killed most of it: the effect was −$7.55/trade in the first window and −$2.42 then −$2.19 after. Net of fees, the recent windows are −$1.23 and −$0.97 — statistically zero.

The mechanism was visible in the calibration. Early on, positions sold at 77.4¢ settled 91.2% of the time — a 13.8¢ gift that never recurred. Recently they sell at 74–77¢ and settle 77–81%, a ~3¢ gap that is approximately the trading fee.

Survives
Take-profit is roughly EV-neutral before fees; it costs about the fee.
Died
The $691 headline. It was one lucky window.

An anomaly that has so far survived every attack

One entry filter — cheap underdogs, 25–50¢, ≥11¢ claimed edge — picked sides that won 52.6% of the time at an average executable price of 36.6¢. That is 16 points better than the price paid, CI [7.6, 24.4].

It is not a midpoint artifact: the entry price equals the ask actually lifted on 194 of 206 trades, median spread 1.0¢, zero midpoint matches. It is stable across three time folds, and a decay regression returns a slope indistinguishable from flat.

Its filter was tuned in ways nobody recorded, and its profit rests on one week. So it is either the one real thing here or an ordinary case of a filter tuned until it looked good.

What it is probably not is model skill. Measured on this filter's own population for the first time — n=1,685, tour, spread ≤10¢ — the model's log-loss is 0.7920 against the market's 0.6628. It loses by 0.129, roughly 3× EXP-8's tour-wide gap. Its mean is well calibrated (+0.0023 bias); its discrimination is worse. Split by whether the bot actually entered, that inverts:

Method

The rules that make the results above worth anything.

Executable prices, never midpoints. EXP-11 was withdrawn because "the market" it beat was the midpoint of a book with a ~80¢ median spread. Every edge figure since is measured against the ask actually lifted.

A market-only control is mandatory. EXP-11 compared blend against model and reversed its own conclusion once the market arm was added.

Sample size committed before data. Including when the honest number is unwelcome — EXP-19 needs 300 clusters and roughly three months.

Adversarial review before belief. Every positive result is attacked for leakage, look-ahead, empty-book artifacts, selection bias, pseudo-replication and unmodelled costs before it is written down.

Nulls are recorded, not buried. An append-only ledger holds every experiment, including two corrections where my own earlier claim was wrong — a counterfactual method I called broken that turned out sound once the comparison was matched properly.

Paper trading throughout; no capital deployed. Figures are live as of 3 September 2026 and will move. Cohort ROI is computed on capital staked, not on the $1,000 nominal bankroll.
